> Prices can return to where they were. Time cannot. <!--more-->

### Breaking Even

This spring, an investment I had held for over half a year finally returned to around the price I had paid for it. I sold it straight away.

It had spent a long time falling. When I first bought it, I had reasons for expecting it to do well. But once the price began to fall, I neither bought more nor sold. I just held on. Over those months, my attention gradually shifted away from the investment itself and toward the gains and losses in my account: how far below my purchase price it was, when it might get back there, whether I could at least finish without a loss. As soon as the price crossed that line and a small profit appeared in my account, I sold, as though I had finally been given permission to leave. A while later, it rose a great deal more.

The market is full of stories like this. Selling too early and watching the price keep climbing is nothing unusual. What stays with me is how much more real my purchase price had come to feel than it had any right to. The market does not know what I paid. That line exists only between my account and my memory; the price will not stop and wait for me when it reaches that number. Yet throughout those months, as long as it remained below the line, I felt that something was still unfinished. Only when it came back did I feel allowed to leave.

“Breaking even” sounds like a phrase about money. But it also makes a demand on time: erase the minus sign, put things back as they were before the mistake, make it as though the decision had left no consequences. Prices can return to where they were. Time cannot.

Years ago, in a highly volatile market, I nearly wiped out my account by insisting on making back a short-term loss I could have afforded to take. At first I had lost only a little. But I could not bear to end in the red, so I kept trading, using far more leverage than my experience or ability to absorb losses could justify. At one point I was close to breaking even. I could have stopped there. Instead, I wanted to erase the last of the loss. A few days later, a sudden, sharp price swing took almost all the money I had put in.

That experience certainly left me wary, even afraid, of heavy leverage. But I also noticed that the first small loss had never been allowed to become the past. Whether the next trade made sense had become a secondary question. It had also been given the job of repairing the previous mistake. I could not accept ending with a negative number, and so I kept mortgaging the future to the past.

The same pattern has since returned in different forms. Once I am down on an investment, I want to wait until it at least approaches my purchase price before selling. When it does recover, I am liable to sell at once. A number belonging to the past becomes a threshold in my mind. Below it, I am still the person who made the wrong decision. Above it, I am, for the moment, innocent again.

There is ordinary loss aversion here, of course. But what I want back always seems to be a little more than money. I also want back the person who had not yet made the mistake, who had not yet been marked by that minus sign.

I have not broken even on every investment, nor could I. Some high-risk trades ended in heavy losses, and afterward my greatest regret was not cutting those losses sooner. Other investments recovered to my purchase price after I had sold, and sometimes climbed higher still. Then regret faced the other way: why had I not held on a little longer? The investment I began with was a third case: I finally broke even, sold immediately, and watched the price keep rising.

Taken together, these experiences do not yield a rule that will always look right afterward. Cutting a loss does not guarantee a better result; I might sell at a temporary low. It is easier to make it a rule because I can at least decide in advance how much risk to take. One of its costs is that prices will sometimes recover after I leave. Waiting to break even can hardly be a plan in the same sense. My purchase price is only part of my history. The market has no obligation to return to it. “I'll decide once I break even” is often closer to a wish.

### Believing Early

This market rally is not necessarily over. But I have probably missed much of the rapid rise as the market reassessed what things were worth. Had I reached a reasonably steady view earlier and started buying, I would at least have some gains to cushion me now. With money already invested, the question during a market swing is what to do with what I hold. Standing outside, I have to ask the same question at every new high: is it already too expensive?

The two situations feel quite different. Yesterday the price looked high, so I stayed out. Today it rises again, and looks higher still. Months pass, with new highs following new highs, until my initial caution has put me in a position where acting feels harder and harder. By the time I finally decide to buy, the market may really begin to fall. My earlier fear of chasing a rising price then seems justified once more.

Over the past two years, I have indeed been too cautious about this, too slow and weighed down. Every action seems to require a sufficiently complete set of reasons. The market does not wait for my reasons to be complete before it moves.

But “believe early” can easily become another piece of hindsight wisdom. I cannot look back and describe myself as someone who had already seen the opportunity and merely lacked the courage to bet on it. The truth is that I did not believe in it that strongly at the time.

Despite my background in science and technology, I am not especially enthusiastic about technology, and still less convinced that its advance means progress. In my reservations about its expansion, I may stand closer to Laozi or Heidegger. At the same time, I am drawn to a more accelerationist possibility: that AI might push the existing movement of capital and technology into a more radical state of imbalance.

Being interested in a break with the course of history is not the same as having a steady investment conviction. And when I check prices from day to day, the reasons are usually much simpler: it has already gone up so much—is it too late to buy? Might I get a better price if I wait? When a pullback finally comes, I wonder whether a bigger fall has only just begun. Laozi, Heidegger, and accelerationism recede into the distance. What remains is a person watching prices change, unwilling to accept that they might fall as soon as he buys.

So I did not really believe then, and I did not act.

What is worth keeping in “believe early” may go no further than this: for a judgment to become an action, it cannot wait until all uncertainty has disappeared. An early conviction can of course be wrong. Buying early can certainly lose money. This only points to something I have always found difficult to accept: when we act, the evidence is still incomplete.

### Back and Forth

A costly trading mistake last year took things in another direction. The market had fallen sharply after a change in policy. I had already begun adding to my long-term investments and had a plan to keep buying gradually. After reading a great deal of bearish analysis, I rapidly reversed my view. I not only abandoned my original position but made a leveraged bet in the opposite direction. The market then recovered. Having made my choice, I kept finding reasons to stay with it, and it was a long time before I finally got out.

The trade cost me real money. But what weighed more heavily was the money I imagined I might have made: how much more would I have now if I had simply followed the original plan and held on? Money that had never entered my account was recorded, in regret, in the same ledger as my actual losses.

For a while, I explained the mistake as arrogance. Pessimism offers an appealing position: other people see only the rise, while I see the risks they have missed; most are swept along by market sentiment, while I remain among the clear-eyed few. Risk is always present. Someone good at analysis can always find reasons to remain cautious. Seeing dangers that others do not see can itself bring a quiet sense of superiority.

But saying now that I have finally recognized my own arrogance is still another way of placing myself above someone. My past self thought he saw more clearly than the market; my present self thinks he sees through his past self. A more mature, more perceptive image of myself quickly takes the old one's place. There really were risks then. My concerns were not groundless. Had the market gone the other way, I would probably be writing a different explanation of myself today. The outcome has no right to travel back in time and declare every doubt I had then foolish.

A short-term view in which I had limited confidence was given the power to overturn a long-term plan. The position I took was also larger than the reliability of my judgment warranted. Reducing everything to arrogance as a character flaw may obscure this more specific problem in how I acted.

The circumstances of my other trades differ, but together they have a similar rhythm: my confidence falters when prices fall; once I am losing money, I do not want to leave; when I finally get close to breaking even, I rush out; when prices keep rising, I regret not holding on. I used to put all this down to risk aversion. But my difficulty is closer to an inability to live with ordinary uncertainty that stays unresolved: being down a little after buying, finishing a trade with a loss, still not knowing where the market stands. To bring these open cases to a quick close, I sometimes make more extreme moves instead.

In a few recent trades, this cycle has played out in a very short time. I buy, then immediately worry that I could have paid less, that the price might fall further, so I sell. No sooner have I sold than I wonder whether I got out near a low, and buy back in. The first transaction price immediately becomes an anchor. When I buy back, I want to pay a little less than I just sold for. When I sell again, I want to get a little more than I just paid. Otherwise, those few minutes of hesitation seem to turn straight into a booked loss.

There is certainly some price anchoring here. But again, it is not just a wish to make a little more. Buying back above the price at which I sold feels like admitting that the sale was wrong. Selling below the price at which I bought feels like admitting that buying was wrong. The second move should ideally correct the first, and do so on slightly better terms. But the market does not keep a place open where I can take back a decision for free.

Hesitation has a cost, just as resolve does. Buying means accepting that the price may keep falling. Selling means accepting that it may rebound immediately. When my judgment genuinely changes, getting back in may require paying more. A decision is a decision precisely because it closes off some possibilities.

The first thing this reveals is a lack of both a clear process and conviction. Why I am buying, what facts would change my mind, what fluctuations I am willing to bear—none of these is clear enough. Price itself then becomes the strongest evidence, and every movement is enough to put a decision I have just made back on trial. Yet calling this a problem with my investment process still seems to leave something unsaid. I find it difficult to let a decision stand before it has been vindicated. Before acting, I feel that the evidence is insufficient. After acting, I want the market to confirm my judgment quickly. When confirmation does not come, I want to take the decision back, preferably at no cost.

Seen this way, waiting months to break even and changing my mind repeatedly within ten minutes may not be so far apart. In one case, I wait for the market to carry me back to where I started. In the other, I begin looking for a way back almost as soon as I have acted. After a setback, earning more sometimes recedes into the background. Restoring the original state becomes urgent instead.

The line marking my purchase price gives this wish a particularly tempting form. The price really might come back, so time seems as though it might come back too. But even if my gains and losses return to zero, the original decision has not disappeared.

### In Practice

What I can actually change remains limited. Before buying, I need to accept that my judgment will not be fully proven. After buying, I need to let it stand for a while through ordinary fluctuations. When the facts genuinely change, I also need to bear the cost of changing my mind. That might mean cutting a loss, buying back at a higher price, or watching a recovery after I have sold. The next action should no longer be responsible for making back the losses of the last. Nor does it need to prove that the previous decision could have been undone for free.

This will not stop me from losing money. It may not help me make more, either. To treat it as a way of finally avoiding mistakes would only move my ideal self to another place: from the person who always calls the market correctly to the person with perfect discipline who never loses control. The aversion to error would remain, only in a more mature guise.

The next time an investment recovers to around my purchase price after a long period of losses, I will probably still hear that familiar “at last.” I may still want to sell immediately. I do not intend to pretend that the price I paid means nothing to me. I only want to close the page first and ask again a day later: if I did not already own this investment, would I buy it today, at this price, knowing what I know now? And if my judgment has changed, am I also willing to bear the cost of changing it?

That line will still be there in my account. But perhaps it will be only an old price, no longer a place that time will let me return to.
