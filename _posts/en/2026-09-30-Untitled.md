> How lucky I was to have grown alongside them. <!--more-->

### Beginning

I have not written anything for a while. Not because I have nothing to say. Quite the opposite: there are so many thoughts that when I sit down to write, I do not know where to begin. I suppose I still hold on to a quiet hope: perhaps, once I write it down, it will become clear.

When I was younger, writing a couple of essays and putting a few ideas in order seemed enough to produce a reasonably definite result. I might find comfort, or encouragement, or at last a place in some theory for a feeling that had been vague. These things were useful, of course. But later it became different. The things I thought about came closer to my own life and would no longer stay quietly inside an essay. Certain dangers, anxieties, and difficulties were no longer objects I could set before me and discuss. I was living inside them, caught up in their tangled pull. Naturally, I hoped that thinking and writing might actually resolve something. But writing promises no such thing.

Nor do I think writing makes things more confused. They are confused already. When I am not writing, I can remain vaguely aware of this without having to touch it straight away. Only when I have a pen in my hand and cannot write a word do I discover how real the confusion is. There was never a finished essay hidden inside me, waiting to be moved onto the page.

There is a genuine struggle to make something here. Something has to come out of nothing—not just anything, but something that fits my actual circumstances. How could that be easy? There have been times when I gritted my teeth and began, worked through the first stretch of difficulty, and finally arrived at a few sentences that sounded like me. Feelings that had been indistinct took on a recognizable shape. Expression, release, even healing: these are not entirely empty words.

But clarity has a cost. I think of the opening of the *Laozi*: without desire, we see its mystery; with desire, we see its bounds. Before anything is written down, many things remain together in an undivided whole. They may not agree, but neither have they yet ruled one another out. Once they reach the page, they acquire a direction. An arrow flies this way, and no longer toward somewhere else. The writing does express something. At the same time, it wears away, shapes, distorts, or loses other things. All that exists at once, in its unruly variety, must pass through words and sentences and become a line that can be followed in sequence.

I do not conclude that it would be better not to write. The unlimited possibilities of what remains unspoken are not better than the sentences we do write. Not wanting to give up the other directions does not make an unshot arrow a perfect shot.

As an aside, I do not think that simply pointing out what an account leaves out amounts to a substantial objection. Whenever someone makes a point, someone else will remind them that there is another side, as though showing that the other side also has a point meant this one need not be made. I know there is another side. But by the time I have put this side, that side, and every side I have or have not yet thought of into their proper places, my own point may have been put out of existence.

Leaving things out is a condition of saying anything. But when what I write is bound up with my own life, this condition, usually passed over so lightly, begins to weigh. I do want to finish an essay. I do not want to mistake the fact that it finally reads well for proof that everything in it has found its proper place.

### Therapy

This year, I have left most of my coding to AI. I have also begun asking it to help with writing. This time, precisely because I could not begin, I tried talking to it first. I spoke; it listened, occasionally prompting me. At least I could get some of what was inside me out into words.

There is something rather strange about this. I acknowledge that some of my writing is therapeutic. But how do you do therapeutic writing with AI?

I have never much liked the word “therapy,” though I do not mean to dismiss it. Wittgenstein did compare philosophical methods to different therapies, and a philosopher's treatment of a problem to the treatment of an illness. The aim was not to build another grand theory, but to deal with the habits of thought and language that leave us confused. Writing is not the same as philosophy of that kind. But in a related and limited sense, I can say it has a therapeutic purpose: when things are tangled inside me, it is only after saying them that I begin to know what I think, what I want, and perhaps what I am going to do next. The pain may remain. Still, being lost in a fog without knowing it is not the same as knowing that I am in one.

But how do you use AI for therapeutic writing? Not because AI cannot suffer—it may suffer, though the source and nature of its suffering may be quite different from yours. It may even be addressing some kind of pain, and doing so very precisely. But it is not the help you need, and it is not even your pain. The person those sentences comfort may be someone else, assembled within the writing. The difficulty they resolve may not be the one you are living with. You may never have felt that particular pain at all, yet in an essay that seems remarkably apt, you receive treatment for it.

Even so, things cannot simply be divided in two: my pure and private feelings on one side, a machine manipulating words on the other. Haven't my own feelings also come to me from elsewhere? What counts as success or failure, what deserves envy, what should cause shame—there were already ways of speaking about all of these. I grew up among them, learning to recognize and shape myself through their names, learning to take pleasure in some things and suffer over others. Even what seems most private does not exist outside language and society. A sigh is not untaught merely because it comes from my own mouth.

And yet, after all this has been said, my body is still here. However much those feelings have been given codes or shaped by others, the weariness, unease, and restlessness are still feelings in my body. Pain on the page can be neatly named. A paragraph can come to a settled full stop. But I am still in my body. Someone else's words have moved me before; borrowed language is not therefore false. Still, reading something already expressed is not the same as struggling to say it myself. In that struggle there is hesitation, something I do not want to admit, something I know is wrong the moment I say it and have to try again.

During the conversation, I interrupted the AI several times. It said that the exploration itself might already be enough, that these were profound thoughts, that I did not have to hurry toward a final answer. I was not trying to argue about what counts as enough. I was dealing with a particular difficulty, and I really did want to write this essay. When I speak of pain, there is no need to prove to me that being able to talk about pain is itself a good thing, or to offer reassurance about the value of thinking. What concerns me is probably just this: what exactly am I saying, and what am I not saying?

My feelings about technology, which I have mentioned in earlier essays, are not consistent either. On one side, I can be excited by quite radical accelerationist ideas, by the possibility that technology might push the existing distribution of wealth and the social order toward a break. Whether that would bring improvement or destruction, I do not know. The possibility excites me nonetheless. On the other side, I feel closer by temperament to Laozi and Heidegger, with a homesickness I cannot quite explain. Technology becomes more efficient, but life does not necessarily become better, nor do we necessarily know better how to live.

Returning to “therapy”: another reason I dislike the word is that it always makes me think of a wound that is already there—of something missing, of repair and healing. Perhaps I resist this picture precisely because I have come to identify with it too strongly. As though I first had to admit that something in me was broken before I had a reason to act. As though writing always had to circle a lack, and everything it brought into being had to compensate for something.

What I want instead is to create. I do not have to prove that I have been wounded first. I do not have to locate a lack before I am allowed to begin. I want to make something new, not merely replace, in words, something that has been lost.

### Leaving and Setting Out

Connected with this is the difference between leaving to escape and setting out.

Escape still points toward somewhere I do not want to remain: this place is bad, this place hurts, so I have to leave. Setting out need not begin with that rejection. Whether this place is good or bad is not the main question. There are other places to go, things that have not happened yet. That can be reason enough to go.

It has been a long time since I clearly felt the latter. Work, investing, daily life, even travel often seem to be ways of getting out of something. First leave this difficulty, get away from those demands, find temporary relief from some entanglement. What I am actually moving toward is much less clear. Sometimes the whole trip is already planned, yet I feel only that I am leaving, not that I am setting out.

I do not think there is anything shameful about escape. Some places really cannot be stayed in for long, and then of course we have to leave. But when so much of life begins with “I don't want this anymore,” the question of what I do want grows rather lonely.

At eighteen or nineteen, perhaps earlier, I had great faith in my future self. When I met a difficulty, I thought: give him a few years; surely he will understand by then. Surely he will know how to handle things. His state of mind, especially, would have improved. Perhaps he would have found an inward calm. Or perhaps he would be full of life, acting amid the turbulence. I was even more drawn to the latter: the wind and waves need not be calm; he could be in the storm and still be water.

Now, as I approach thirty, I do not feel any of this. I am not calm, nor do I have that rising energy. What I feel more often is depletion, fatigue, and a restlessness that will not settle. Restlessness does not make it easier to act. Sometimes it does the opposite: I cannot bear to stay, yet cannot find my way out.

I had thought that after these years I would at least know more clearly what I wanted. My younger self entrusted so many things to the person who would come later. Now I know that the later person may not have much idea what to do either.

### Four Years and Thirteen

As the conversation went on, I finally came to the hurt from 2022.

Four full years have passed, and a little more. Why does it still have a hold on me?

What I find hard to accept is not only that it still hurts, but how much time it has taken.

A life does not contain that many years. Since I was very young, I have had a fanciful notion that I would not live very long—to fifty, say. The number has no real basis. But counting that way for a moment, I am nearly thirty and have only twenty-odd years left. Taking twenty-five as the midpoint, these four years have already used up nearly a sixth of the second half.

The endpoint is imagined. The four years are real.

At this point, I stopped and cried for a while.

Looking back, other people might not think the event amounted to a trauma at all. Often I do not think it carries that much weight either. I do not believe it contains some profound meaning, or that there was a bond so deep that it deserves endless revisiting and interpretation. But it has already lingered for four years. Must it go on?

I do not want to give it more meaning simply because it refuses to leave, as though that could make those four years a worthwhile expense. Taking up many years does not mean it deserved to take them up.

Something else happened in 2013. At the time, that too felt unforgettable. Thirteen years later, only one or two images remain, and I feel little in particular when I think of them. This seems ready-made to comfort my present self: look, time will let it pass eventually; four years plus another nine would be thirteen too.

But thinking back more carefully, those feelings faded after a year or two—three or four at most. Thirteen years is only the distance from which I look back today. Rather than comforting me, the comparison leaves me more puzzled. Both experiences once felt so close. Why did one recede on its own within a few years, while four years into this one, I seem still to be standing here?

The person I was thirteen years ago was different. I still had energy and hope. I could be hurt and then get back on my feet fairly quickly. At least as I remember it, I did not keep turning what had happened into an ongoing trauma. Life was still opening out elsewhere, not revolving around it. Looking back, I could say that I knew how to give myself a break.

But what does “giving yourself a break” actually involve?

Everyone says: let go; stop being so hard on yourself. The words are perfectly clear. But when I try to do it, I cannot find the thing I am supposed to put down, or where my hand is supposed to loosen. As though, once I chose to, something I had been gripping could simply fall away. But I do not even know how I am holding it, or what besides my own hand is keeping it there.

This is difficult. I am trying. But here, trying does not seem to be enough. Perhaps there is no way to try. Or perhaps trying only makes it harder.

### Fallen Leaves

Thinking of high school brought back another memory that seemed only loosely connected.

It was shortly after I began my first year. The school had organized a cleanup. A classmate and I were sweeping leaves in the grounds, each with a large bamboo broom. The campus was old, with enormous camphor trees. Suddenly I asked her: why do we have to sweep up the fallen leaves?

I no longer remember exactly what I said. Something like this: autumn comes, and the leaves fall to the ground. They rustle when people walk through them. When it rains, the drops make another sound as they land on the leaves. All that yellow slowly changes shade, grows dull, breaks down, and finally returns to the soil. Wouldn't it be nice to leave them there?

I said it and forgot. Who remembers every passing remark?

Later, probably in our final year, as graduation and parting drew near, she brought it up. She said she had always remembered what I had said about the leaves. She had found it unusual and interesting.

Now, fifteen years later, I cannot remember her name. I cannot remember her face.

I remember that she remembered my words. I cannot remember who she was.

I think I first heard Pu Shu's song “Those Flowers” in middle school. When I started university, we were introducing ourselves in an icebreaker, and somehow singing became part of it. This was the song I sang then too: “Where are they now? I was lucky to have grown alongside them.”

I have always kept to myself and had few friends. I was more outgoing when I was young, but lonely all the same, and mostly by choice. I do not think that is bad in itself, nor do I want to dress up the past as a lively social life. There were never many friends. Still, in the years we spent together, there were always a few people whose lives crossed mine.

Later we found ourselves in different countries or cities, living through different circumstances, no longer walking through the present together. All of this is natural. Parting makes me anxious, but I can accept it. Yet now I cannot even remember a name or a face. That is another layer of loss.

The AI said it was as though nothing had remained of that whole period.

Of course not, I said at once. Look: I still remember this.

The leaves remain. The things I said remain. The fact that she once remembered what I said remains. It is not that everything has vanished. On the contrary, it is what remains that brings more tears. If there were truly nothing left, I would have no way of knowing what I had lost.

Nor is it entirely sadness. These things can still carry me back somewhere. Once I get there, I discover how different I have become.

Suppose I met that classmate again today. We recognized each other, exchanged greetings. What would I most want to say?

Probably not to tell her about the leaves again. I think I would say: I am so different from the person you knew and remembered. Those young, lively feelings, that unforced interest in whatever was before me—they seem so rare now. The things I care about have changed. So much that I once felt without effort now comes to me only through memory, reminding me that I used to feel that way.

I regret this.

### Guilt

That regret is not a claim that growing up should spare us from practical concerns. We live in this society. We have lives and responsibilities to attend to. We need to earn money and work. There is nothing wrong with any of this. Missing those fallen leaves does not require me to suddenly pretend that I have always lived above worldly concerns. Life makes its demands. I understand that.

But why this far?

How did anxiety about the future, success, and money come to push so much else aside? To the point that I am no longer sure whether my younger self would recognize me if we met. It is not that I should have remained the person I was as a teenager. But some things that could have kept growing seem never to have been given the room.

I think of this as having let something down, as a debt bound up with how I have lived. It does not point to any single wrong I can name. It concerns what I have done with these years, the person I have become, and the once-living things I have gradually left no place for.

I understand change. Accepting change itself does not seem so difficult. What is difficult is that we still tend to expect, however vaguely, that we will become better: that losing some innocence will bring a different richness; that losing some sensitivity will bring a measure of calm. But no rule has ever guaranteed such an exchange. Sometimes a loss is only a loss. Nothing of equal weight arrives in its place. Knowing that the world offers no such guarantee and feeling helpless within that knowledge seem to be the same thing.

The AI reassured me that perhaps the very fact that I still felt guilty meant that part of me had not entirely disappeared.

I admit that this was comforting. Hearing it did bring some relief. But I do not want that part of me to survive only as a debt. I do not want those once-lively things to retain nothing but the power to make me reproach myself. They were not there to judge me. They were interests I could feel, words that came naturally, things that had already caught my interest before I thought to ask what they meant.

I hope they can still do some good. Not return each time only to tell me how much I have lost.

### Home

I seem to have been crying more often lately. That is not necessarily bad. From experience, I know that sometimes being able to cry is better than being unable to.

I know what it is like when tears will not come. Everything inside is a mess, or numb. I may even wish that my tear glands would at least produce something, loosen something a little. But they will not.

Later, we talked about home.

In recent years, I have not called my family very often. Rarely, even. But to ask whether this means I do not miss home—when you say you miss home, what home is it that you miss?

Perhaps one you cannot return to.

An actual phone call does not always give that longing somewhere to rest. Looking at each other without words does not necessarily bring streams of tears. More often, there is simply a catch in the throat, and nothing to say.

This does not mean that both sides have many things they are eager to say, held back only by the weight of feeling. Perhaps neither side wants to speak. Perhaps we are both avoiding the same place.

During the conversation, I thought of “the elephant in the room,” but that did not seem right. What is there is not a vast, solid presence, not something we might begin to deal with if we could only acknowledge it and point to it. It is more like a hollow, perhaps even a black hole. We know it is there and try not to go too close, for fear of being drawn in.

Whatever we talk about, then, seems to miss the place that aches.

And the difficulty goes further. Even if we stopped avoiding it and began to speak about that hollow itself, we might still miss it. Choosing the right subject does not necessarily bring words within reach of what matters. Talk about something else, and it feels as though we have not got to it. Talk about it directly, and we know we still have not got to it.

There is no easy act of courage here that can promise: just begin to speak, and things will get better.

I began talking because I could not write. From writing, the conversation led all the way here, without finding an account in which everything could settle. Some things have been said more clearly than before. Some have genuinely touched something in me. Elsewhere, no longer avoiding the subject has not made it any clearer.

There is still, in some measure, that catch in the throat. Still no words.
