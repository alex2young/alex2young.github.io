> We tend to compare ourselves only with people we might have become. <!--more-->

### Making Money

Perhaps gains, losses, and choices carry so much weight because they have never been about money alone. They point toward other outcomes that never happened: mistakes not made, gains not missed, a career in which I did not stay where I was, and the person who chose differently at some crossroads and came to have a different life within reach.

Why is money so well suited to holding these unlived lives? I also wonder when I began to care so much about it. I used to think I had little interest in money when I was young and only grew more eager to earn it after a few years of work. Looking back, that story is not quite right. I wanted to make money early on. The wish simply did not always show itself in the same way. When I saw an opportunity, it would suddenly become vivid. After a setback, it would retreat for a while, as though I had never cared much about money. Then another opportunity would appear, and the desire would return.

So “not being very interested in money” may not have been detachment. It may simply have been a desire that had sunk out of sight after being frustrated.

When I was young, I did imagine living with very little. Looking back, I did not want poverty itself, nor was I willing to bear its real consequences for housing, health care, aging, and family responsibilities. What appealed to me was a picture of a life: money would recede into the distance; I would earn a reasonable amount, enough to cover everyday expenses, and turn my attention back to reading, writing, travel, play, and living. There was probably some self-flattery in this too, as though keeping money at a distance would make me freer.

By now, perhaps I should put it more plainly: money really matters. It means security, and the ability to choose and to leave. With more money behind me, I would be less anxious in a job I disliked. I could better afford to stop and rest, change careers, or refuse an arrangement I did not want. In adult life, freedom depends on actual conditions. Without money to support it, “I can leave whenever I like” is often just a sentence.

I am also no longer willing to explain the desire to earn money as some base impulse I ought to overcome. I simply want to earn more. Seeing my assets grow makes me happy. Things become complicated when money takes on functions beyond supporting a life. The most obvious of these is comparison.

### Comparison

We rarely compare ourselves with everyone. I do not often think of people who are spectacularly successful in business or very rich and feel a failure as a result. They do not readily become my reference points. The people who affect me are usually close enough to resemble me: people with similar educations, colleagues in similar circumstances, friends of roughly the same age with comparable career paths. Precisely because we once stood close together, the distance between us now seems measurable.

I think of classmates who went to work for technology companies. In the early years, I may have earned more. Since then, with stock compensation and rising markets, they may well have accumulated more wealth. This is partly guesswork. I do not know their actual incomes, spending, or investments. But comparison does not require complete information. Some colleagues joined startups, quickly took on larger responsibilities, and happened to be at companies that did very well. Their roles, incomes, and equity all grew with the companies. If a stranger makes a fortune by starting a business, I am likely to think he is capable and lucky. But when someone who once worked alongside me, who may even have half-jokingly asked me to come with him, goes a long way after making that move, a question readily appears: why didn't I leave?

The companies that are successful today did not have those outcomes written into their job offers. Some people really did recognize an opportunity earlier, accept the risk, and earn a return. Staying where I was had its reasons too: familiar work, a steady income, concern about uncertainty, and no clear sense of what to do next. It was a choice I made. I cannot tell the story as though I played no part in missing the opportunity. Besides, the ways my career has fallen short of my expectations cannot all be traced to one job I did not take. I have not always been fully committed to my work. My enthusiasm for the career itself has often been weak, and my sense of direction has wavered. There were things I could have tried for and did not. At some crossroads, I stayed because moving seemed a nuisance, because I feared the risk, or because I simply did not want it that much.

Yet judging myself as someone who “didn't try hard enough” may be just as simplistic. Who gets a project, who happens to notice a person's work, how an organization judges it, when an opportunity appears, what a decision yields years later—much of this is beyond an individual's will. Looking back, these tangled circumstances can easily be compressed into a few simple sentences: I should have worked harder; I should have left; I should have taken that opportunity. As though life had presented clear right answers at certain points, and answering correctly would have brought me somewhere else today.

Things were probably not that clear. Knowing this does not make the road I never took any less appealing. Precisely because I never traveled it, none of its risks, doubts, exhaustion, or accidents ever happened. In retrospect, it can look unusually straight.

Comparison takes other forms in investing. Some people really are better investors than I am. They have a more mature understanding of markets, individual investments, how much to put into them, and the risks, and have earned better returns. Others spend little time on research and do not constantly try to predict the economy. They simply stick with a basic passive investment plan and, over the years, have done quite well too.

The first group makes me question my ability: why can't I judge things better? The second makes me question my urge to judge at all: why do I have to make so many judgments? Together they raise a third question: why didn't I act sooner? These reproaches do not agree with one another. One tells me to be cleverer, another to stop being so clever, a third to be more decisive. Yet they do not cancel one another out. They can all stand at once.

Perhaps they can coexist because I am not always comparing myself with one whole person. The people I envy differ: one invests more skillfully, one follows a simple plan, one joined a successful startup, another stayed at an established company and built up substantial wealth through long-term compensation. There may well be someone who is more successful than I am in career, income, and investing, and more at ease as well. There would be nothing surprising about that. But whether he actually exists is not really the point.

### Imagination

I do not need to see anyone's account or establish how much anyone has earned. I only need to imagine that someone starting from a place like mine saw the opportunities sooner, made fewer mistakes at crucial moments, and now enjoys more. That position is already established in my mind. A classmate, a colleague, someone I happen to hear about, or an imagined version of myself who did not sell, reverse course, or stay put—any of them can occupy it. Someone in real life might come close to it, or even occupy it fully. But the question was never really whether such a person exists.

This is the place reserved for someone who seems to have all the enjoyment I lack: there is always someone who knows more, makes better choices, and has more to enjoy. I put my ideal self there—cleverer, more decisive, richer, and more at ease. I also borrow his point of view and turn it back on the person I am. I want to become him, and I live under his gaze.

Here, fantasy shapes how I understand opportunity, failure, and time. As long as I believe that someone in similar circumstances could have made better choices, uncertainty quietly changes its nature. The market seems always to have had a right answer. My career seems always to have offered a better route. He saw it; I did not. Chance is recast as ability. A fork in life's road becomes an error of judgment. Regret gradually becomes a case against myself.

The roads not taken are especially liable to acquire this power. They have no actual terrain, none of the incomplete information, risk, fatigue, or hesitation of the time. Only the destination, now known, remains. The roads we never took have no journey to complicate them, so they always look straighter than the roads we did take. Judged backward from today's destination, an uncertain path becomes a question I should have answered correctly.

Of course, analysis cannot undo the facts. Some people really are better investors than I am. Some really did seize opportunities I failed to take. Had I adopted a simple, steady investment plan earlier, I might well have more money today. What psychoanalysis can do here is limited. It cannot dissolve these differences into fantasy. It can only reveal how I move from particular, real differences toward a position occupied by someone who always does better than I do, and then hand my actual self over to his judgment.

### Not Enough

Money is particularly suited to carrying these comparisons because it comes in numbers. Once a vague unease enters a calculation, it looks remarkably precise: how much less I earned in a certain year, how much a decision cost me in missed opportunities, how much more I might have now if I had taken another road earlier.

Gradually, these numbers form an invisible ledger. It no longer keeps track only of money, but of how I have lived these years. My salary becomes a score. A colleague's possibly higher salary becomes a score. Someone joins a startup and their equity rises in value: another score. Someone earns more by following a simple investment plan: another. Money begins to answer questions it was never able to answer: am I clever enough, have I succeeded, have I let myself down, have I missed the life I might have had?

In practical terms, even a substantial sum arriving soon would not immediately transform my life. Losing the same amount would not plunge me straight into hardship either. Yet the feeling of not having enough has not receded as my assets have grown. How my career may change, what responsibilities my family may face—these are real questions for which money provides a buffer. But money has also been made to prove something. If it served only to pay for a life, we could probably discuss a reasonably clear sense of “enough.” Once it must also prove whether I am clever enough, whether I have fallen behind, whether I have missed too many opportunities, the numbers struggle to reach an endpoint.

The place reserved for that other person moves forward as my assets grow. Even if I reach a figure I now imagine, he may still be a step ahead through an earlier career choice, a better investment, or a more effortless life. The position endures precisely because it is not identical with any particular person I could overtake. The harder I try to catch up in numbers, the more new measures I give it.

I want to make money largely because money means freedom. But if calculations about investments, comparisons, and missed opportunities keep taking over my attention—if I am tallying assets, costs, and gains and losses at work, over meals, and while traveling—money begins to take away the freedom it was meant to provide. “Care less about money” will not resolve this. Money really matters. Career choices have consequences. Investing is part of adult life. Prolonged avoidance, waiting, and half-participation have real costs too. Pretending none of this matters would only construct another flattering image of myself as being above it all.

### The Road I Have Taken

The roads I never took will probably continue to look straight in retrospect. Some regrets are real. I do feel that something is lacking, and I do feel guilt. But those roads have no journey left in them. I am still on mine.

They have left me with regret. They do not have to keep making today's decisions. What I actually want now, and what I am willing to take on, can only be answered here, on the road I am walking.
