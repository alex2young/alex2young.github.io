### Contemplation

In *The Secret of the Golden Flower*, Jung writes about a rather strange difficulty. He says that some people, in making progress and freeing themselves, in fact “did nothing”: they simply “let things happen.” Drawing on Meister Eckhart, he speaks of releasing our hold on ourselves, and finally arrives at the need to “let things happen in the psyche.” But this does not make things easy. It is precisely where the difficulty begins. The conscious mind always steps in to interfere, help, correct, or reject. First it dismisses the images that arise on their own as foolish, dull, or pointless. Once that has been overcome, it cannot help explaining them, sorting them into categories, making them beautiful, or belittling them. Jung even calls this a “cramp of consciousness,” and speaks of the need to curb the conscious mind's impatience.

I am probably someone who is used to thinking. When a thought appears, I find it difficult simply to notice that it has appeared. Having got this far, I feel I should think a little further. Once I have found a cause, I want the cause behind it. With something painful, this is even more like unfinished work: perhaps another round of thought will produce a different explanation, perhaps even resolve it at last. But many things have not become clearer because I have thought about them more. They have merely been thought about again.

This also makes me think of contemplation in Buddhism. Of course, these are not the same thing. The Chinese text behind *The Secret of the Golden Flower* has its own Daoist context, and Jung is interpreting it from another setting again. But at least the contemplation I hope for is not the kind of looking inherited from Greek and European thought, where something is placed before us and closely examined. Nor is it seeing things as a whole in the sense of Goethe or Wittgenstein. In Buddhist terms, one could speak of seeing how things arise through conditions, recognizing that they have no fixed existence of their own, and clinging to nothing; then looking again from that emptiness and seeing the world arise in all its variety. All of this can be explained fluently, even profoundly.

But when a thought comes up, how exactly do I “observe” it? If I try to contemplate “myself,” I seem to need to maintain a clear, stable self, while thoughts become things that keep arriving to disturb that position. If I turn to contemplate the thoughts instead, I easily follow them wherever they lead. The difficult thing is to stop.

Interestingly, the Chinese text is very specific. On looking at the tip of the nose, it says to “take a single look when first entering stillness, establish a guide, then let it go.” This is like a mason hanging a line: “he does not keep staring at the line.” When a thought arises, one should “examine where it is, where it comes from, and where it passes away.” But after examining it repeatedly and finding nothing to grasp, the text adds: “do not go looking for its source again.” Finally: “Let stillness be followed by observation, and observation by stillness.”

I do not yet really understand the practice being described. But “do not go looking for its source again” interests me. It does not mean that we should think about nothing; the preceding sentence explicitly tells us to examine it repeatedly. Yet there seems to be a point where further questioning is no longer observation but the conscious mind taking over again. We always feel there must be another layer, and another beneath that, as though thinking long enough would finally bring us to an explanation with nothing left over. Perhaps this is what the mind's impatience amounts to.

### The Thunderstorm

Jung offers another image I like. Some problems, he says, are not resolved by working through their own logic. Instead, as life takes a new and stronger direction, they gradually lose their hold. From a higher point in a person's development, conflicts that once felt so fierce can look like a thunderstorm in a valley, seen from a mountaintop. But he immediately adds that, inwardly, we are the valley as well as the mountain. The storm has not disappeared. It can still shake and torment us. At the same time, we can say: “I know that I suffer.”

“To be in a fog without knowing it, and to know that one is in a fog—these are more than a thousand miles apart.”

I long for this state. Of course, even “longing for it” is a little dangerous. The conscious mind can so easily turn it into another goal to achieve: after enough practice, I will finally live at the summit forever, never again going down into the valley to get caught in the rain. Jung seems already to have closed off that route. We are both the mountain and the valley. Of course I want the pain to stop, but I also know that this is not very realistic. Perhaps, then, I can suffer a little less over the fact that I am suffering. Or, to put it another way: I can suffer and also be happy.

And so I come back to Jung's phrase: “let things happen in the psyche.”

### The Doctor

I also think of the Tenth Doctor in *Doctor Who*. He seems especially unable to stop thinking. It is not only that there is always someone waiting to be saved. This really is how he lives: a slight anomaly, an unfinished sentence, a detail he cannot explain—any of these is enough to set his mind racing. Yet, strangely, he is also always traveling, from one planet to another, from one adventure into the next. Sometimes I think these may be the same thing. As long as there is something new to think about, he need not stop to face the things that thinking cannot resolve. The Time War, loss, loneliness, farewells—none is a puzzle that yields to a little more thought. And the universe is vast. The TARDIS can always take off again.
