> We should regard the beautiful moments of life as grace. <!--more-->

*On reading Wittgenstein*

I have found Wittgenstein difficult in a way that is not quite the difficulty of an obscure writer. Often I can follow his sentences, explain his examples, and even anticipate the misconception he is about to expose. Yet I remain unsure whether I have understood what matters. The explanation seems to leave me almost where I began.

A Chinese phrase kept occurring to me: to reach the utmost height and yet follow the ordinary way. What is difficult, in reading him, is to perceive the height. His conclusions can sound so unremarkable that one wonders why it took philosophy to arrive at them. Knowing how to play chess does not require a rulebook to pass through the mind whenever one says “chess.” Looking for someone does not establish that the person exists. A picture does not carry its application within itself. These are things I can readily agree with. But agreement is cheap if I have not felt the attraction of the picture being questioned.

Perhaps something similar happens when I read his ethical remarks. I recognize a clarity in them, and recognition briefly feels like participation. Then I notice how differently I live.

My questions while reading Chen Jiaying’s *Wittgenstein Reader* began with explanation: what does Wittgenstein mean when he calls it an illusion to suppose that natural laws explain natural phenomena? The issue became more interesting when I stopped treating this as a contest between science and something supposedly higher. Scientific explanations need not be discredited for the demand placed upon them to be questioned. What troubled me was the point at which an explanation begins to promise more than an account of what happens—when it seems to offer a final resting place for thought. Wittgenstein’s comparison between modern people stopping at natural laws and earlier people stopping at God and fate concerns, among other things, our failure to recognize where we have stopped. (*Tractatus*, §§6.371–6.372.)

There is a personal temptation here. I may ask why something happened because an answer would help me act. But I may also ask because I want the event to become acceptable. I want an explanation to accomplish a reconciliation. If I could understand enough, perhaps what happened would cease to feel arbitrary; perhaps I could finally agree to the life in which it happened.

Those are not the same request.

Wittgenstein’s remark that all possible scientific questions might be answered while the problems of life remain untouched does not direct me toward a more comprehensive science. Nor does it license a rival body of knowledge, concealed beyond the reach of ordinary inquiry. It unsettles the assumption that a sufficiently complete account of things would settle my relation to them. (*Tractatus*, §6.52.)

This is where his explicit ethics begins to matter to me. In the *Tractatus*, he separates ethical value from the contingency of what happens, and ethical reward from the ordinary consequences of an action. Whatever one makes of these severe formulations, they resist turning goodness into a bargain: act rightly, and life will eventually compensate you. (*Tractatus*, §§6.41–6.422.)

I can reject such a bargain in principle and still find subtler versions of it in my expectations. Perhaps I do not expect virtue to bring happiness. Yet I may expect seriousness to bring clarity, self-knowledge to bring peace, or a sufficiently considered choice to protect me from regret. Even philosophy can enter this arrangement. I give it my attention; in return, it should make my life come out right.

I begin to suspect that some of my disappointment with thought comes from asking it to honour a promise it never made.

***

The later remarks on meaning and rules approach a different set of questions. I do not want to flatten the differences between Wittgenstein’s periods into a single doctrine of relinquishment. Still, in following these investigations, I encounter a recurring demand that has ethical force for me: to distinguish what I can actually account for from what I feel compelled to add.

When I grasp a rule, its future applications seem already to be present in my understanding. This need not mean that a present psychological event causes everything I will later do. But once that causal account is rejected, a more elusive picture offers itself: perhaps all the applications are present *in some strange way*. Wittgenstein’s response is remarkably exact. He does not simply forbid us to say that the applications are already present. He questions the added strangeness. We have given an intelligible expression an imagined operation unlike its actual use. (*Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics*, I, §126.)

The example of the child puzzled by a tailor “sewing clothes” makes the mistake almost embarrassingly visible. The child imagines the entire garment being produced by sewing alone, thread by thread. There is nothing mysterious about the tailor’s work. The mystery belongs to the picture through which the child has understood the words.

I find this more demanding than a general injunction to avoid metaphysics. It asks me to locate the precise addition I have made. What in the matter itself is difficult, and what difficulty have I introduced by insisting that it must work in a certain way?

The passages on proof sharpen the question. A proof is not an experiment whose repeated success makes us increasingly confident. Yet neither does the bare display of a symbolic transition provide its own ultimate justification. Wittgenstein asks what happens when a proof becomes a model, when its conclusion acquires a new place in our calculations and judgments. His distinction between a movement and a path stays with me: a proof is not merely an occurrence we have witnessed; it is something we can return to and use. (RFM III, §§39–41.)

None of this establishes an ethical conclusion. Mathematical necessity is not the same problem as my wish to feel secure. But I recognize a resemblance in the temptation: I want something beyond the actual grounds, something that will guarantee those grounds without itself requiring anything further. A meaning behind every use; a guarantee behind every guarantee; eventually, a justification for the whole of life before I can consent to any part of it.

The implicit ethics I find here is an exacting honesty about that desire. It concerns the point at which thought ceases to clarify and begins to protect a demand from examination.

This honesty cannot be reduced to saying that everything is ordinary, or that problems disappear once words are properly used. A loss is not necessarily a misunderstanding. An accurate description may make grief more intelligible without making it less painful. But I can still ask what happens when I move from saying that something has failed to saying that my life is a failure, or from saying that something has ended to treating its ending as the truth about everything that preceded it.

The words remain familiar. The work I have made them do has changed.

This may be part of the height concealed in Wittgenstein’s return to the ordinary. He does not merely recommend smaller claims. He makes it possible to notice where a claim has acquired authority over me without my quite knowing how.

***

Then I came to this passage from the wartime private notebooks:

> Even the nearest future cannot be known with certainty. In short, there are moments in which I can not merely live in reality, but live for the spirit. We should regard the beautiful moments of life as grace, enjoy them with gratitude, and otherwise be indifferent to life.  
> —NB II, p. 24

My immediate response was that this was profoundly right, and that I did not inhabit the attitude it expressed.

The word *grace* changes the relation between a good moment and the rest of life. A good moment can easily become evidence: things are improving; the choices were justified; perhaps the future will now continue in this direction. Even while enjoying it, I may begin to ask what it secures. Its value becomes entangled with its promise.

To receive it as grace would be to enjoy it without making it answer for what comes next.

This is harder than appreciating something temporary. I can acknowledge that a moment will pass while secretly requiring that it leave behind a permanent entitlement—a reason to expect its return, a reassurance that life is ultimately favourable, something I can retain when the moment itself is gone. Gratitude, as I hear it here, does not obtain that reassurance. It responds to what has been given without converting the gift into a claim.

The moment’s passing would still be a loss. But its passing would not retrospectively cancel its goodness.

There is a religious resonance in *grace* that I should not quietly translate into a technique for well-being. The sentence does not say that gratitude is an efficient way to feel better. Nor does it establish gratitude as the rational consequence of uncertainty. An uncertain future can provoke fear, defiance, calculation, or resignation. The movement from uncertainty to gratitude is not a deduction. Wittgenstein is articulating an orientation toward life, not proving that everyone must adopt it.

What moves me is that enjoyment remains possible within this orientation. He does not ask that beauty become less beautiful so that its loss will hurt less. The moments are to be enjoyed. The difficulty lies in allowing them their full importance without requiring them to become permanent possessions.

The final words are more troubling: “otherwise be indifferent to life.”

I do not want to make these words comfortable too quickly. Indifference can be freedom from a demand that the world conform to my wishes; it can also be an accomplished name for withdrawal. There is a difference between declining to make my happiness conditional upon every outcome and declining to care about outcomes at all. Other people’s suffering cannot become unimportant merely because I have found a more tranquil relation to my own.

The reading I am drawn to is therefore also a question I must keep open. Can I care deeply about what happens without requiring it to justify existence as a whole? Can I work to change a circumstance without making its change the condition under which life will finally be worth living? The distinction is intelligible to me. I cannot claim that it remains clear when something I care about is at stake.

Perhaps “living for the spirit” names, for me, those moments when my concern is not exhausted by the question of what events mean for *me*. This is a reading rather than a definition. Its difficulty is that I cannot achieve it merely by instructing myself to be less self-concerned. The instruction can become another occasion for self-inspection.

***

The distance between recognizing these words and living them brings me back to the discussions of understanding.

To understand “chess” is not to experience a compressed presentation of all its rules. Nor is the explanation I give of what I meant necessarily a report of something that passed through my mind. The explanation belongs to the life of the expression; it need not have accompanied its use as an inner event. (*Philosophical Grammar*, I, §13.)

There is an uncomfortable analogy here. Being moved by an ethical sentence is a real experience, but it is not yet the life the sentence brings into view. I may feel, for a moment, what it would be like to relinquish a demand. That feeling does not mean I have relinquished it.

At the same time, I would be too severe with myself if I concluded that I had understood nothing. There is a difference between recognizing a possibility and being able to sustain it. The first can expose the absence of the second. That exposure is part of what reading has done.

But it would be easy to turn this absence into a fresh indictment. Now I must also become someone who receives beauty without possessiveness, faces uncertainty without fear, and no longer needs reassurance. Detachment becomes another accomplishment to secure. I could pursue freedom from self-demand in precisely the spirit of self-demand.

This does not mean that no effort is called for. It means that I need to notice what kind of effort I am making, and what I expect it to purchase.

It also matters that the notebook passage speaks of *moments* and of what we *should* do. I have no reason to read it as a report of uninterrupted serenity. Its clarity does not certify that its author always lived within it. To turn the sentence into the portrait of a perfected person, and then judge myself against that portrait, would be to add another picture to the words.

Elsewhere in the anthology, Wittgenstein asks what use philosophical study has if it does not improve our thinking about the important matters of everyday life. Thinking honestly about one’s own life and the lives of others, he says, is harder than thinking through difficult logical questions; it may be distinctly unpleasant rather than intellectually exciting. (NMM, p. 33.)

That remark prevents me from making his ethics merely a way of becoming less troubled. The issue is not only whether I can cease demanding reassurance. It is whether I can become more truthful about what I want, what I am doing, and what my actions ask of other people. A quieter mind is not, by itself, an adequate answer.

This is what I now hear in the phrase that first occurred to me: to reach the utmost height and yet follow the ordinary way. The ordinary is not a place where nothing matters very much. It is where the distinctions have to be made—in a judgment, a conversation, an expectation, an act of attention—without the protection of a theory that has already made them on my behalf.

I remain drawn to the possibility of a comprehensive understanding that would settle me once and for all. Even this essay can serve that wish: arrange the thoughts well enough, and perhaps their arrangement will become a way of life. But the ability to describe a change is not the change itself.

For now, the most exact thing I can say is that Wittgenstein has helped me see a demand I do not yet know how to relinquish. I want what is good to remain, and I want its remaining to assure me that life is good. The notebook passage allows these wishes to come apart.

Something was good; it has ended. I would like to be able to say both without making the second sentence retract the first.
